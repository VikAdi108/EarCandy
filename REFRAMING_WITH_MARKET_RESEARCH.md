# Reframing with market research

September 2026

## The direction

EarCandy steers people into music they would not have found on their own. For the listener, that cuts boredom. For the musician, it solves the problem of getting heard. The market research behind this (September 2026) found that both sides have the same problem from opposite ends: there is too much music, and the systems that sort it keep people inside what they already know, especially inside their own language. Mood has no language, so mood can be the bridge.

The biggest unknown is how many listeners actively want music from other cultures, as opposed to accepting it when it is offered. No published survey measures this. This document is the plan for finding out, in a way that also tells us how to build the product better.

## Why not a plain questionnaire

A plain questionnaire will mislead us. If you ask people "would you like to discover music from other cultures?", nearly everyone says yes, because it sounds like the good answer, and then they go back to their usual playlist. So the test should watch what people do with the app and ask only about what they have actually done in the past. It should not ask what they think they would do.

Run it in three parts with the same 20–30 people.

## Part 1: a ten-minute conversation before they touch the app

All the questions are about real past behavior, not opinions:

- "Tell me about the last time you found a new artist you still listen to. How did that happen?"
- "When did you last feel bored with your music? What did you do about it?"
- "Do you ever listen to music in a language you don't speak? How did you come across it?"
- "What do you pay for music now?"

If many people can't remember the last time they were bored with their music, the boredom problem is weaker than the research suggests. That is worth knowing before building further.

## Part 2: they use the app while we watch

Give them one task: "Pick how you feel right now and listen for 15 minutes." Someone sits with them, or the screen is recorded. The app should quietly log a few things per song:

- Did they skip it, and how many seconds in?
- Did they hit the heart?
- Was the song in a language or style outside what they normally play?

The number to look for is whether unfamiliar songs get skipped more than familiar ones. If people skip foreign-language songs at about the same rate as the rest, mood works as the bridge between languages. If they skip them twice as often, it doesn't work yet, and the way unfamiliar songs are introduced needs to change.

Two cheap experiments fit inside this part:

- **How much unfamiliar music per playlist.** Give half the people a playlist with about 20% unfamiliar music and the other half about 50%. This shows how hard we can push before people leave.
- **A one-line reason on each song.** Show half of them something like "this is a Malian guitarist, picked because it's calm and bright", and show the other half nothing. Spotify is betting that explanations matter, and this tells us whether they matter for EarCandy.

## Part 3: a check one week later

Leave the app with them and check back after a week with three questions:

- Did they open it again without being asked?
- Did they save or look up any artist they found through it?
- Did they tell anyone about it?

What they do on their own during that week tells us more than anything they say on the first day.

## Who to recruit

Don't use only friends and family, because they will be kind. Aim for a mix:

- some heavy listeners who make their own playlists
- some casual listeners who just press play
- a few people who already listen across languages

Classmates cover the younger group; recruit a similar number of adults. Keep a note of which group each person is in, because the most useful finding is often "this works for one type of person and not another."

## What each result tells us to build

| What we see | What it means for the product |
|---|---|
| Unfamiliar songs are skipped about as often as familiar ones | The core idea works. Lean into it. |
| They're skipped much more, but less when a reason is shown | Build the "why this song" line into everything. |
| They're skipped much more either way | Introduce new music more gently, for example one unfamiliar song between two safe ones. |
| People enjoy the session but don't come back in the week | The app is pleasant but not needed. It needs a reason to be opened daily, such as a morning mood check. |
| People look up the artists afterward | The musician side has a real basis, because listeners do go deeper on artists they find this way. |
| Nobody recalls being bored with their music | "Reducing boredom" is the wrong pitch. Look at what they did complain about in Part 1. |

## The musician side

This needs conversations, not an app test. Talk to 8–10 independent musicians, ideally several who sing in a language other than English. Ask what they did to promote their last release, what it cost in time and money, and what came of it. Then describe the idea and ask what they would want to get out of it: more plays, more followers, or fans who pay them directly. Don't build anything for musicians until the listener test shows people actually follow through to artists.

## What has to be built first

The app needs to record skips, hearts and listening time per song before any of this can run.
